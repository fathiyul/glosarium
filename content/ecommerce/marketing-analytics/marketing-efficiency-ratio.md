MER = total revenue ÷ **all** marketing spend, across every channel, in the same period. Where [ROAS](/t/roas) asks "what did this campaign return?", MER asks "what did the entire marketing machine return this month?" — and because it sums everything, it is far harder to flatter.

```text
paid social ROAS 5× · search ROAS 7× · email "12×" · affiliates 4×
sum of claims: every channel excellent
total revenue / total spend: 1.9×   ← someone is lying, usually quietly
```

That gap is why MER earns its place in the growth review. Channels claim overlapping credit: the same purchase counted by Meta, by Google search, by the email platform and the affiliate ([attribution](/t/attribution) double-counting is structural, not sloppy reporting). Sum of ROAS claims routinely exceeds 100% of your revenue; MER can't be double-counted because revenue is counted once.

Founders at growth stage: MER plus payback period disciplines the "scale what works" instinct. Dropping MER across two consecutive months while every dashboard glows green is the oldest signal that budget is being moved *between* channels (harvesting shifts) instead of *creating* demand. The healthy use is trend and direction — your own history, spend changes visible — not a target number ripped from a benchmark post.

Analysts: watch MER's numerator trap. Total revenue includes organic, repeat and seasonality demand, so MER *falls* when you cut spend into a strong-retention base (the revenue was never bought) and *rises* in a promo quarter that inflated both sides. Read it alongside [burn multiple](/t/burn-multiple) and cohort repeat rates; when spend steps change, compare like windows.

When not to use it: early experiments (total spend too small, MER is noise), and any argument where a channel's specific ROAS actually answers the question. The honest pairing: MER for budget-level truth, holdout experiments for channel-level truth ([holdback group](/t/holdback-group)), attribution for path-level color.
