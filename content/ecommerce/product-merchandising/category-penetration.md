Category penetration = customers who bought in category C during a window ÷ all active customers. It measures *breadth* of demand, distinct from revenue share (how big) and from [frequency](/t/purchase-frequency) (how often): beauty at 8% share might be bought by 35% of customers as a small add-on, or by 1% of whales. Same revenue, completely different business.

```text
customers buying category in 12mo ÷ active customers:
  phone accessories 61%   — commodity, wide, thin margin
  small appliances    9%  — considered, seasonal
  beauty              27% — growth target: cross-sell from 27 → 35%
```

The two decisions it drives:

- **Growth sizing.** Lifting penetration of an existing category among *existing* customers is usually cheaper than acquisition — the [segmentation](/t/segmentation) view shows which customers buy everything-but-your-category; the gap is the campaign.
- **Assortment defense.** A category's penetration trend is its relevance trend — accessories dropping from 61% to 52% with flat revenue means concentration (fewer buyers, bigger baskets), which is fragility, not strength ([long-tail contribution](/t/long-tail-contribution) is the twin diagnostic).

The analyst notes: state the denominator (*active* customers? everyone-ever? in-market buyers?) — penetration against the wrong base is a different metric wearing the name; watch **window effects** (a Ramadan-only category shows ~0% outside its season — penetration for seasonal goods is "among the eligible window," not annual); and penetration ≠ profit — a high-penetration loss-leader category is a traffic strategy, priced somewhere else in the [waterfall](/t/profit-waterfall).

The natural pair is [cross-category rate](/t/cross-category-rate): penetration per category tells breadth, cross-category tells whether breadth *connects* — a store with five 30% categories whose buyers never overlap is five stores sharing a checkout.
