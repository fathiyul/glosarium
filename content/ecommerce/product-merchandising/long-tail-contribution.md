The long tail is the shape of e-commerce demand: a small head of SKUs with heavy volume, a huge tail with sparse orders — and the question that drives the metric is how much revenue (and *profit*) the tail carries, because holding it is not free.

Measure concentration, then ask if it is *earned*:

```text
top 10% of SKUs        → 72% of revenue (head)
next 40%               → 24%
bottom 50%             → 4% — how many units per year per SKU?
                         carrying cost: storage, ops touches,
                         dead-stock risk, search relevance noise
```

Tail economics, the honest version: each tail SKU that sells even monthly still costs shelf space, cycle-count attention and listing maintenance; a thousand 2%-turn SKUs can quietly outweigh a hundred 20%-turn ones. The tail *earns* when it serves a discoverable purpose — the "we have exactly your part" reason a specialist store wins search ([long-tail SEO queries convert fiercely](/t/category-penetration)), the cross-sell attachment ([attach-rate](/t/attach-rate) on hero items), the marketplace where selection itself is the product. It fails when it was added for a hoped-for margin no one searched, priced without [elasticity](/t/price-elasticity), and never cleared.

The operational lens pairs with [ABC/XYZ](/t/abc-xyz-analysis): tail-C/erratic-Z is where "stock one, ship from supplier, print-on-demand" policies beat warehouse economics; tail-A is impossible by construction — check the classification if you find it.

Analyst cautions: concentration numbers (Herfindahl-style or simple top-N share) need a *defined* unit — SKU vs variant vs SPU; and platform catalogs where sellers self-list need seller-concentration checked separately (marketplace health is its own view). Finally, revenue share is not contribution share: run the tail's [margin-per-SKU](/t/margin-per-sku) before defending it — thin-head, fat-margin tails change the answer completely.
