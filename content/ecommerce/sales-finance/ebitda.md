EBITDA = operating profit + depreciation + amortization — the profit your business generates from operations before capital structure (interest), the taxman (tax) and accounting write-downs of past investments (D&A). For e-commerce founders it becomes a familiar number in exactly one moment: when an investor, lender or acquirer asks "what's your EBITDA?" — or when you benchmark growth-stage software and marketplace valuations on EV/EBITDA multiples.

The reason it exists: two identical stores, one that bought a warehouse and one leasing, report different net profits purely because of depreciation. EBITDA peels that decision away to compare *operations*. It is a normalization habit, not a purity claim.

Where it misleads precisely, which is why analysts should never present it naked:

- **"Non-cash" today is cash yesterday.** A warehouse, vans and a WMS implementation all left the bank account. Add-backs are a *view*, not free money — a capital-intensive retailer with rosy EBITDA and negative free cash flow is the classic EBITDA trap.
- **Adjustments.** "Adjusted EBITDA, excluding one-off founder comp / launch costs / stock-based comp" is a genre of creative writing. Every add-back needs a defense in the appendix.
- **Not a substitute for [cash-flow](/t/cash-flow)** — EBITDA ignores working capital, and e-commerce is *made of* working capital: inventory buildup before Ramadan, refund float, COD settlement lags.

When EBITDA is the right lens: comparing your operating efficiency across quarters despite capex timing; a credible milestone for pre-exit conversations; stress-testing whether the [profit waterfall](/t/profit-waterfall) has a bottom line at realistic scale.

When it is not: [break-even](/t/break-even) decisions for the next warehouse purchase (that's D&A territory you *chose* to ignore — buy the machine, eat the depreciation; EBITDA flatters you while the asset starves cash), and any argument where a reviewer has the power to ask "adjusted how?" — pre-empt them.
