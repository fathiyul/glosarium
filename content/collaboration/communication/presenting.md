---
title: "Presenting"
summary: "Communicating ideas to a group."
levels: { backend: senior, frontend: senior, data-engineer: senior }
---

Presenting is organizing information so an audience can understand a topic, evaluate a choice, or take action. A technical presentation is not just a tour through slides or code; it should answer a question that matters to the people listening.

Start with the audience and desired outcome. A design review may need the constraints, alternatives, recommendation, and open questions. A project update may need progress, risks, and decisions required. For example, show the data flow that explains a migration risk rather than reading every implementation detail aloud.

Keep evidence connected to the claim, and distinguish measured results from assumptions. Leave time for questions and make it easy to find the follow-up material. Slides can support a conversation, but a durable written summary is useful for people who were absent or need to revisit a decision.

Backend, frontend, and data engineers should translate their technical choices into effects on users, operations, and other teams without hiding important caveats. Avoid overloading a broad audience with implementation detail; offer a deeper appendix or discussion for specialists. See [effective meetings](/t/effective-meetings), [async communication](/t/async-communication), and [stakeholder management](/t/stakeholder-management).

Choose the channel and amount of detail for the people who need to act, then make important outcomes findable later. If a conversation changes scope or ownership, record that change rather than relying on memory. Backend developers can explain service impact, frontend developers can clarify interaction concerns, and data engineers can make lineage or measurement implications visible.

After trying it, ask whether people had enough context to act and revise the agreement if it created extra coordination or left someone out.
