**Jobs to Be Done** says customers don't buy products — they *hire* them to make progress in a specific situation. People hire a drill for a hole, a hole for hanging a shelf, a shelf for an organized home. The job, not the product category, is the unit of competition: your rival is everything hired for the same job, including spreadsheets and interns.

```text
job: "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]"
e.g. "When month-end closes, I want reconciled books without chasing receipts, so I can report on time"
```

Framing work this way changes what you build: features serve the job's struggling moments, onboarding targets the hiring moment, and churn analysis asks which competing "hire" won. It also explains why better products lose — the incumbent was hired for reliability and habit, not features.

The classic mistakes:

- Jobs so broad they explain nothing. "Help teams collaborate" fits a thousand products. A good job names the situation, the struggle and the desired outcome specifically enough to design against.
- Ignoring the firing moment. Every hire implies a fire — the old workaround abandoned. If you cannot name what gets fired, adoption has no trigger and the sale never closes.
- Persona theater. Demographic personas ("Marketing Mary, 34") without jobs attached decorate walls and decide nothing. Attach every persona to its jobs or drop the persona.
- Assuming the job is static. Jobs evolve as situations change (remote work rewrote thousands). Re-interview around the job yearly; the product that stops matching the job starts churning silently.

**Use it for:** positioning ([value proposition](/t/value-proposition) in job language converts better), onboarding (first run should complete the core job), and competitive analysis (list all hires for the job, not just direct rivals).
