A **pivot** changes a core element of strategy — customer segment, problem, solution, channel, revenue model — while keeping the team, learnings and some assets. It differs from flailing (random changes) and from quitting (abandoning everything): a pivot is a *structured* change of hypothesis based on validated learning that the current path will not work.

```text
zoom-in pivot:   one feature becomes the product · customer-segment pivot: same product, new buyers
platform pivot:  app becomes platform · business-architecture pivot: high margin → high volume
```

Pivot on evidence and once, decisively: the data says this segment will not buy, this channel will not scale, this model will not monetize — so change that element completely rather than hedging two strategies at half resources each.

The classic mistakes:

- Pivoting without a verdict. Changing direction from restlessness, a competitor's launch, or investor fashion rather than from invalidated beliefs. No learning went in; none comes out.
- Half-pivots. Keeping the old product alive "just in case" while starting the new thing splits focus and confuses everyone. Commit resources fully or do not pivot.
- Pivoting the team away. New direction needing entirely different skills means new hires while old ones idle. Staff the pivot honestly, including painful exits.
- Too many pivots. Each pivot spends credibility with team, investors and customers. Two genuine pivots is normal; five is thrashing — at some point the pattern, not the idea, is the problem.
- Confusing pivot with perseverance failure. Some things need pushing through (slow enterprise cycles, seasonal troughs), not pivoting away from. Distinguish "wrong path" evidence from "hard path" discomfort before turning.

**The question:** "What did we learn that invalidates the current plan, and what specific change follows?" Asked and answered in writing, a pivot is strategy. Otherwise it is panic with better branding.
