**Product-market fit (PMF)** is when the product satisfies a real market so well that demand pulls it forward: users come, stay, pay and bring others, faster than the team can fully serve. Marc Andreessen's classic line — "the market pulls product out of the startup" — captures the feeling founders describe: struggling to keep up rather than struggling to convince.

```text
pre-fit:   push (convince each user, churn constantly, growth needs effort)
fit:       pull (word of mouth, retention flattens, servers strain, hiring lags demand)
```

There is no single metric, but the [retention curve that flattens](/t/retention-curve) is the closest thing to proof: cohorts that stabilize instead of decaying to zero mean lasting value. Supporting signals — [Sean Ellis scores](/t/sean-ellis-test), organic growth share, sales cycles shortening — corroborate; none substitutes.

The classic mistakes:

- Declaring fit early. A launch spike, a big logo, a press wave — all mimic pull temporarily. Fit is sustained across cohorts and quarters, not a moment ([false fit](/t/false-product-market-fit)).
- Scaling before fit. Hiring sales and spending on acquisition into a leaky product multiplies losses, not growth. Fit first, fuel second — premature scaling is the classic way to die with money.
- Averaging into fake fit. One strong segment plus nine dead ones reads as "some traction" overall. Fit lives in segments; find which ones pull and double down there.
- Ignoring the feel. Founders in fit report being overwhelmed by demand and broken processes; founders pre-fit report convincing and chasing. Trust the qualitative signal alongside metrics.
- Stopping discovery at fit. Markets move, segments saturate, competitors arrive. Fit decays without continued discovery — the loop continues, only the questions change.

**After fit:** shift resources from searching to scaling — the company reorganizes around growth ([growth stage](/t/enterprise-sales)), and the founder's job changes from finding truth to building the machine that exploits it.
