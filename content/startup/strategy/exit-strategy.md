An exit strategy is the answer to "how does the illiquid equity everyone holds eventually become money?" — the planned paths being acquisition by a strategic or financial buyer, an IPO ([ipo](/t/ipo)), secondary liquidity sales ([secondary-sale](/t/secondary-sale)), or long-term dividend operation without a sale at all. Investors model exits before they invest; founders who cannot name a plausible path are implicitly saying "I know what I'm building but not why it's worth more later."

The main doors:

```text
strategic acquisition:  buyer wants product/market/team; often
                        pays for synergy — needs clean IP,
                        contracts transferable, tidy cap table
financial buyer / PE:   buys cash-flow predictability; wants
                        audited numbers, management depth
IPO:                    public-market liquidity; years of
                        audited reporting, governance
secondary / never:      partial liquidity now; or a company
                        that pays dividends indefinitely
                        (a fine life — but tell investors)
```

The mistake pattern: building with zero exit legibility. Not optimizing for sale — but if *no plausible buyer or market could want this*, fundraising is a treadmill. The second mistake is the reverse: over-optimizing for acquisition so hard you build something only one acquirer wants, and negotiate from weakness when they know it. [Startup acquisition](/t/startup-acquisition) prices your leverage honestly.

What makes exits *possible* — decided years earlier, not in the process: clean [cap table](/t/cap-table) with disciplined [vesting](/t/vesting-schedule), IP properly assigned, [financial audits](/t/financial-audit) and revenue-recognition that survive diligence, contracts transferable without consent gymnastics, and single-person dependencies removed from operations (a business that only the founder can run has one buyer's problem: they must buy the founder's calendar).

The founder-level truth: exits are a *milestone* in most venture math, not the meaning of the company; and the timing asymmetry — buyers negotiate hardest when your runway is short ([runway](/t/runway) is exit leverage). The best exit posture is the one where you do not *need* the exit; build it in that direction from the first board meeting ([board of directors](/t/board-of-directors) conversations include this path) and the strategy takes care of itself.
