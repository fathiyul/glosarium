Seven Powers is a strategy framework (Hamilton Helmer's book) for the durable advantage a business builds — seven specific *kinds* of moat, each with its own mechanics and timing, so "we have a moat" becomes a falsifiable claim. The list:

```text
1 network economies      users attract users, value grows with
                         scale of the connected set
2 switching costs        leaving takes money/time/data/risk
3 cornered resource      exclusive access to talent, IP, supply,
                         location, regulation
4 counter-positioning    a model rivals can't copy without hurting
                         themselves
5 unique product/process/brand     preferred offer or reputation
                         price can't be matched
6 scaling advantages     cost-per-unit falls with volume, rivals
                         can't reach it
7 process power          learned, embedded operations competitors
                         can't replicate (manufacturing, ops
                         muscle)
```

The framework's real edge is *sequencing*: which power is buildable from your stage and capital. Early companies usually get unique product/brand first; network economies need density; counter-positioning needs an incumbent with a margin to defend. Building the wrong power too early — chasing network effects in a category that doesn't produce them — is the expensive error the list makes visible.

The founder mistakes: treating features as moats (product power is a moat only if it resists replication — most don't), and counting one power where several are required — [switching costs](/t/switching-costs) without network economies is a retention story; [economies-of-scale](/t/economies-of-scale) without protection is an efficiency the competitor buys later.

It is a map, not a formula: the diagnosis stays judgmental, and markets vary — for most SEA startups, realistic early powers are brand, switching costs (workflow, integrations, data lock-in) and process power, with network economies reserved for genuinely connected products.

Use it in [due diligence](/t/investor-due-diligence), board conversations ([board of directors](/t/board-of-directors)) and your own planning as the honest question: *"name the power, and what stops rivals from buying it next year?"*
