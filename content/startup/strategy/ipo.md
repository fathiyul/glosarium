title: "IPO"
summary: "Listing the company's shares on a public stock exchange."
levels: { tech-entrepreneur: scale }
---

An IPO is the first sale of a company's shares to the public — liquidity for holders, a currency for acquisitions, and admission to permanent quarterly judgment. For most startups it is the distant exit path rather than the near one, but the readiness habits it demands are worth borrowing years early.

What the process actually involves: choosing banks/underwriters, a prospectus filing (audited financials under the listing jurisdiction's rules, a narrative for public investors, risk disclosures), a roadshow selling the story to fund managers, pricing, then the first trade — followed by lock-up periods (insiders cannot sell for months), quarterly earnings cycles, and disclosure obligations that become the company's operating rhythm. Timelines and mechanics differ by exchange; the shape above is the general one.

The founder reality-check before wanting one:

```text
an IPO needs:    years of audited, public-grade financials
                 governance the board already enforces
                 a business the public can understand in
                 one page — and growth that does not
                 depend on the founder's calendar
an IPO costs:    ongoing legal/compliance/IR overhead
                 permanent scrutiny of every quarter
                 loss of the private-market ability to
                 take long hits quietly
```

Common motivations read honestly: real (access to cheaper capital, brand credibility, employee liquidity at scale); and self-deceiving (fleeing acquisition negotiations, founder exit optics, "the next milestone" treated as destiny). There are alternatives with most of the liquidity and fewer of the chains: late-stage [secondary sales](/t/secondary-sale), a strategic [acquisition](/t/startup-acquisition), or dividend-funded independence.

The readiness argument founders actually control: if you ever might list, build the muscles now — [audited](/t/financial-audit) numbers that hold, a [board](/t/board-of-directors) that functions without the founder, reporting a public market could digest. It makes every other path better too, which is why even exit-pessimists should act IPO-ready. And note the gatekeepers: markets accept companies at listing windows and sectors — timing is a variable you do not control, so treat the IPO as an option you become worthy of, not a schedule.
