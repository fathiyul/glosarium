---
title: "Aggregation Theory"
summary: "How internet platforms win by owning the user relationship and commoditizing suppliers."
levels: { tech-entrepreneur: scale }
---

Aggregation theory is a model of internet value (described by Kevin Kelly) arguing that when supply is abundant, customers consolidate their attention into a few aggregate experiences — and the *aggregator*, not the suppliers, captures the surplus. You search (Google aggregates content), you ride (an app aggregates drivers), you shop (a storefront aggregates sellers). The aggregator owns the customer relationship and the data; the suppliers compete for its ranking, commoditizing themselves.

The logic chain:

```text
internet makes distribution abundant
→ the scarce thing becomes customer attention
→ customers bundle into the best aggregate experience
→ the aggregator's leverage over suppliers grows
→ value concentrates at the aggregation layer
```

For founders the reading is a positioning question with two edges. Upward: if you sell into a channel that aggregates, your relationship, data and margins are hostage to its priorities ([platform risk](/t/platform-risk) is the same coin) — build your *own* direct relationship and aggregate your category where a fragmented supply genuinely needs a better consumer experience (the classic [two-sided-marketplace](/t/two-sided-marketplace) move). Downward: the theory explains why "great product, no distribution" founders keep getting squeezed — being a supply node in someone else's aggregate is structurally weak, no matter the quality.

Where the theory overclaims: it underestimates suppliers fighting back (brands leaving marketplaces, apps building owned demand), aggregation's own fragility (antitrust scrutiny, switching to the next aggregate), and regulated/local categories where aggregation cannot simply import — many [SEA](/t/regional-expansion-sea) markets split by country and constraint precisely because local trust, payments and logistics resist global bundling.

How to use it honestly: as a map of *where leverage accumulates* in your market. Ask who aggregates your customers' attention, what you own that they cannot replicate, and whether your growth plan amounts to renting scale (aggregator-dependent) or building a position ([seven-powers](/t/seven-powers): switching costs, cornered resources, network economies) where you are the aggregate. The theory is descriptive, not destiny — but acting as if distribution were neutral is how good products become suppliers to someone else's business.
