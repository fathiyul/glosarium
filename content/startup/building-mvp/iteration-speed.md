**Iteration speed** — idea to users to learning, per week — is the startup's structural advantage over incumbents. A five-person team can ship daily, watch usage hourly and change direction weekly; a large company needs quarters for the same loop. Speed does not guarantee learning, but slowness guarantees losing to someone faster.

```text
advantage:  your loop: days · incumbent loop: quarters → you get 10× the tries
spent well: tries aimed at riskiest assumptions · spent badly: churning UI trivia
```

Speed comes from scope discipline ([cutting scope](/t/scope-cutting)), boring technology ([stack](/t/startup-tech-stack)), deploy automation from day one, and decisions made by the people with context — not from heroics and all-nighters, which buy one fast week at the cost of the next six.

The classic mistakes:

- Speed without learning. Shipping daily with no measurement and no decisions is hamster-wheel agility. Every cycle must end in a conclusion (see [build-measure-learn](/t/build-measure-learn)).
- Heroic pace. Crunch sustains weeks, destroys quarters. The fastest teams protect sleep, keep scope tiny and automate relentlessly — boring sources of speed that compound.
- Process that slows with headcount. Each hire, meeting and approval adds latency. Defend the loop explicitly as you grow: small teams, clear owners, deploy rights.
- Optimising deploy speed over decision speed. Ten deploys a day of unmeasured changes teaches nothing. The bottleneck is usually deciding and measuring, not shipping — instrument first.

**Measure it:** time from idea to user feedback, weekly. If it grows past two weeks pre-fit, stop and fix the loop before building anything else — speed is the strategy until fit is found.
