**Freemium** gives the product away free to most users and charges a minority for premium features, limits or seats. The free tier is marketing that pays for itself in distribution: users arrive without sales effort, some convert, and the base generates referrals, feedback and network effects.

```text
100k free users → 3% convert → 3k × $20/mo = $60k MRR (numbers vary wildly by product)
```

The math that matters is support cost per free user versus conversion rate. If free users cost real money (support, compute, storage) and convert at fractions of a percent, freemium is a charity. It works when marginal cost per free user is near zero and the upgrade trigger is a natural usage ceiling.

The classic mistakes:

- Free tier too generous. Full product free with a decorative paid tier converts nobody. Gate the value that businesses (not hobbyists) need: seats, admin, integrations, limits.
- Free tier too stingy. A crippled trial disguised as free teaches nothing and converts nobody either. The free experience must deliver real standalone value.
- No upgrade path designed. Users happily free forever because nothing nudges them at the moment of need. Instrument usage ceilings and prompt upgrades contextually.
- Support drowning in free. Free users filing tickets at paid-user rates sinks the team. Self-serve support for free, humans for paid — tier support with pricing.
- Confusing signups with success. A million free users churning monthly is a cost center with good press. Track free-to-paid conversion and free-tier unit economics, not registrations.

**Test it:** free tier with a designed ceiling, conversion instrumentation from day one, and a support-cost budget per free user. See [free trial](/t/free-trial) for the time-boxed alternative and [pricing tiers](/t/pricing-tiers) for structuring the paid side.
