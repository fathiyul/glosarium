The **take rate** is the platform's cut of each transaction: 15% of the ride, 10% of the booking, 30% of the app sale. It translates activity (GMV) into revenue, and small differences compound brutally — three points of take on a billion in flow is thirty million in revenue (and roughly the entire profit pool).

```text
$100 ride → 20% take → $20 platform revenue → minus support/insurance/fraud = contribution
levers:  rate % × volume × mix (high-take categories subsidize low-take growth ones)
```

Set it by value added and competitive alternatives, not by aspiration: too high drives both sides off-platform (disintermediation), too low starves operations. The rate must also flex by category — high-touch services bear more than commodity listings.

The classic mistakes:

- Raising take to fix unit economics. Squeezing suppliers who already earn little accelerates churn and invites competitor entry positioned as "fair". Fix costs or mix instead.
- Flat rate across unlike categories. Food delivery margins and service bookings economics differ totally. Tier by category reality or subsidize losses unknowingly.
- Ignoring leakage feedback. Rising off-platform completion is the market voting the take too high for the value added. Treat leakage metrics as pricing signals, not enforcement problems alone.
- Hiding the take. Opaque fee stacks that sellers discover at payout breed rage and regulation. Transparent breakdowns (service fee + payment + marketing opt-ins) retain trust while capturing value.
- One-way ratchets. Raising takes is easy; lowering them later (competitive response) destroys forecasts built on the high rate. Stress-test strategy against forced cuts before depending on peak rates.

**Review yearly** per category: rate vs value delivered, vs alternatives, vs leakage. The take rate is the business model quantified — small numbers here are the whole game.
