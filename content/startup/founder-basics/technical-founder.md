A **technical founder** is an engineer who starts a company — and discovers the job is no longer mostly code. In the beginning the coding advantage is real: you can ship the first version yourself, talk to users with a demo in days, and iterate without hiring. That speed is the technical founder's superpower.

```text
month 1–6:   you are the engineering team (build fast, talk to users)
month 6–18:  you are hiring your replacement (first engineers, then leads)
later:       you review architecture and hire — the code ships without you
```

What changes is where leverage comes from. An engineer's leverage is output; a founder's leverage is decisions — which problem, which customer, which hire, what to kill. Code you write in month three is rarely the constraint; the customer you have not talked to is.

The classic mistakes:

- Building before talking. Months of architecture for users who do not exist yet. Ship the smallest thing and show it to people (see [do things that don't scale](/t/do-things-that-dont-scale)).
- Hiring yourself. Recruiting engineers exactly like you duplicates strengths and gaps. Hire for what you lack — often sales sense, design, or domain knowledge.
- Staying the bottleneck. Reviewing every pull request at twenty engineers, or refusing to delegate architecture. Your job evolves toward hiring and direction (see [the CTO role as you grow](/t/cto-role-evolution)).
- Confusing technical validation with business validation. "It works" is not "anyone pays". Engineers trust demos; markets trust wallets.

**Your edge, used well:** prototype speed, bullshit detection on technical claims (vendors, hires, outsourcing), and credibility with early engineering hires. Spend it on learning speed, not on perfect code.
