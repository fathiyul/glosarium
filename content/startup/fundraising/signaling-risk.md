**Signaling risk** is new investors reading insiders' behavior as information: the lead passing on follow-on, insiders skipping the bridge, a famous angel conspicuously absent — each whispers "they know something." Whether fair or not, the signal moves valuations and kills rounds that fundamentals might have carried.

```text
insiders in → "people with full information still buy" (strongest endorsement available)
insiders out → "why?" (every new investor asks; reasons better be excellent and shareable)
```

Manage it prospectively, not defensively: keep insiders close between rounds ([updates](/t/investor-updates)), surface problems early while help is possible, and secure soft follow-on commitments *before* shopping externally. A round launched with insiders already in converts interest into momentum.

The classic mistakes:

- Discovering the pass mid-process. Shopping a round for weeks before asking insiders, then watching new interest evaporate on their pass. Insiders first, always — their answer shapes the entire strategy.
- Accepting vague passes. "Not our stage focus anymore" might be true or polite fiction. Push kindly for the real reason — portfolio conflict, partner departure, genuine concern — because each implies different handling.
- Hiding insider dynamics. New investors discover passes independently (small world, direct questions). Disclose with context proactively; discovered passes detonate, disclosed ones merely cost.
- One investor's veto power. Structuring such that a single insider's participation gates everything hands them pricing power over your company. Diversify the insider base before you need any one of them.
- Burning bridges on passes. Today's pass is tomorrow's bridge, acquirer or reference. Thank, understand, stay close — relationships outlast rounds.

**The rule:** no external process without insider clarity first. Their participation (or explained, contextualized absence) is part of the pitch whether you present it or not.
