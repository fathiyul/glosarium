**Running a fundraise** means operating it as a time-boxed sales process: target list built, meetings compressed into weeks (not dribbled over quarters), momentum manufactured through parallel interest, and closing driven to signed wires. Process beats brilliance — mediocre decks with great process outperform great decks with drift.

```text
weeks 1–2:  50+ first meetings (parallel, fast) → weeks 3–4: partners + diligence
weeks 5–6:  terms from leads → leverage into best terms → close + wire
```

Parallelism creates leverage: investors move on deals others want, on timelines that force decisions. Sequential fundraising (one meeting a week for months) surrenders both — no competition, no urgency, endless maybe.

The classic mistakes:

- Dribbling meetings over months. No momentum, no competition, stale story by close. Compress ruthlessly — announce the process window and hold it.
- No pipeline math. Ten meetings cannot yield a round (conversion rates demand volume). Work backwards from needed term sheets through realistic conversion to a big-enough top of funnel.
- Due-diligence unpreparedness. Scrambling for documents, metrics and references mid-process stalls momentum fatally. Prepare the [data room](/t/data-room) *before* the first meeting ([diligence](/t/investor-due-diligence)).
- Negotiating alone. Solo founders with one offer take what is given. Parallel processes plus counsel plus founder-network advice is the minimum viable leverage.
- Announcing prematurely. "Raising!" broadcasts desperation timelines; "closing soon with room" broadcasts momentum. Control information flow — process news travels fast in investor networks.

**Time-box it:** six to eight weeks of intense process, then decide — close, extend deliberately with cause, or pause and rebuild traction. Open-ended raises bleed morale and signal weakness to the exact people being courted.
