---
title: "Secondary Sale"
summary: "Founders or early holders selling existing shares, as opposed to the company issuing new ones."
levels: { tech-entrepreneur: scale }
---

A secondary sale is existing shareholders selling shares to investors — your cash, your employees' early risk, turned into real money — while the company raises nothing new. Early secondaries let angels recycle and seed funds return some capital; late-stage secondaries at big marks let employees who joined pre-IPO get liquidity without waiting for a listing ([IPO](/t/ipo) timelines are long).

The shape of a typical transaction: investors (often a growth fund or SPV) buy founder shares or employee shares at a discount to the latest round price, because they are buying restricted, illiquid, minority positions — and because the company's consent and rights of first refusal filter who may sell. Board and investor approvals, [term sheet](/t/term-sheet) transfer restrictions and [vesting](/t/vesting-schedule) status all gate it.

Why founders do them deliberately: personal financial pressure that would otherwise compromise decisions is real, and a founder who has sold *some* risk keeps judgment; early employees cashing out small amounts of long-held shares stay motivated instead of leaking to jobs that pay cash. A controlled program — employee tender at the company's invitation, modest size — formalizes this.

The traps:

- **Optics.** A large founder secondary before the company proves the next phase reads as "they know something" — to hires, to investors in the next round, and to the remaining employees underwater on their strikes.
- **Tax timing.** Personal gains are taxed where you are, and rules on when and how differ across jurisdictions — the Indonesian, Singaporean and US treatments are not the same ([npwp](/t/npwp)/local counsel for the former, advisor for the rest); the discount structure interacts with what you report.
- **Governance.** Letting a favored few sell when others couldn't creates resentment that outlasts the cash.

When it is right: modest, early enough to relieve real risk concentration, structured transparently with board sign-off. When it is wrong: as a substitute for the company's own health, or when the only buyers are people who will use the information asymmetry in the next negotiation. And never assume "the mark" on a secondary is the company's next round price — discounts are the norm, not a slight.
