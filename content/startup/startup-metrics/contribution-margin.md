---
title: "Contribution Margin"
summary: "What each sale contributes after variable costs, before fixed costs."
levels: { tech-entrepreneur: growth }
---

Contribution margin is what one sale puts toward covering fixed costs after every variable cost of serving that customer is counted: price minus payment fees, hosting, support hours, onboarding effort, commissions. A $100/month customer with $20 in variable cost contributes $80 — the fixed cost of the company (salaries, office, core platform) gets covered 80 customers-at-a-time, not per profit line.

The decision tool is segmentation: compute it per channel, per segment, per deal shape.

```text
SMB self-serve: $100/mo − $18 variable  = $82 (82%)
Mid-market sales-led: $800/mo − $180 (CSM time, commissions amortized) = $620 (77%)
Enterprise bespoke: $5,000/mo − $2,600 (SE hours, custom integrations) = $2,400 (48%)
```

Now the question "should we keep selling enterprise?" has a number attached: each enterprise deal funds 29 SMB-equivalents of fixed cost at a fraction of the margin, with worse cash timing. [Pricing](/t/pricing-tiers) and product decisions follow from the arithmetic.

The classic mistakes: counting only "COGS" and stopping at 80% gross margin while ignoring that sales commissions, onboarding labor and support tickets are variable too — that is how companies discover their best-looking segment is their least profitable. The second is forgetting amortization: a deal desk that costs $60K/year serves the enterprise segment; per-deal variable costs pretend it is free.

Contribution margin differs from [gross margin](/t/gross-margin), which is an accounting view of direct product costs over a period; contribution margin is your managerial view of what one incremental customer adds. Both matter; they are not interchangeable.

When the math is hard, approximate the big movers: hosting per account, support minutes per ticket type, commissions and deal costs. Perfect is the enemy; knowing the top three variable costs per segment already beats most companies. When not to use it: very early (cost structures don't exist yet), and never for one-off deals where "variable" is really one-time investment worth its future value.
