CAC payback period is how many months of a customer's gross profit it takes to earn back what you spent acquiring them. CAC $1,200, a customer worth $100/month at 80% gross margin ($80/month profit): payback is 15 months. It turns [unit economics](/t/unit-economics) into a cash-flow question: how fast does each sale fund the next one?

The metric matters most before you have piles of cash. A short payback means [runway](/t/runway) funds growth again and again; a long one means you are financing customers from the balance sheet, which is what turns growing revenue into growing cash hunger. Venture circles often cite "under 12 months is great, under 24 is workable" for SaaS — treat any such bands as convention, not physics, and compare against your own churn and cost of capital.

```text
blended CAC / gross-margin ARR per customer per month = payback months
$1,200 / $80 = 15 months
```

The classic mistakes:

- Using revenue, not gross margin. Hosted products have real variable cost; paying back on revenue overstates speed.
- Blending everything. Self-serve $30 CAC and enterprise $15,000 CAC average into a meaningless "24 months." Segment by channel; some segments may pay back in weeks while others never do.
- Ignoring the sales ramp. Enterprise CAC includes months of selling; include fully loaded selling cost or your ratio flatters itself.
- Timing games: annual prepay collected day one makes cash payback trivial while the [revenue recognition](/t/revenue-recognition) reality is monthly.

When not to optimize it: at tiny scale, noise dominates; in genuine land-grab markets investors may fund long paybacks deliberately — but you should still know the number, because "we can't tell you our payback" is itself a warning sign to anyone doing diligence.
