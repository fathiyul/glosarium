**Outsourcing development** means paying an agency or offshore team to build the product. It buys speed without hiring and makes sense for well-specified, bounded work. For the core product of a tech startup, it is usually a mistake — the product *is* the company, and outsourcing it outsources learning, quality control and the ability to iterate.

```text
works:   bounded piece (landing page, internal tool, v1 of a side feature) with clear spec
fails:   "build our startup" — core product, evolving spec, learning locked outside
```

When you must (no technical founder, pre-funding prototype), constrain ruthlessly: fixed scope with acceptance criteria, weekly demos against the criteria, code in your repository from day one, and IP assignment signed before work starts.

The classic mistakes:

- Outsourcing the learning. The agency learns your market, users and tech lessons — then leaves with them. What remains is code nobody inside understands. Keep discovery in-house even when hands are rented.
- Spec-and-pray contracts. Fixed-price contracts for evolving products produce change-order warfare. Pay time-and-materials with tight iteration, or fix scope genuinely small.
- No technical oversight. Non-technical founders cannot judge agency code quality, and agencies optimize for delivery appearance. Get an independent technical review before final payments — or a technical advisor throughout.
- IP and access hygiene. Agency staff with production access, no assignment clauses, credentials shared in chat. [IP assignment](/t/ip-assignment) plus least-privilege access from day one.
- Cheapest-bidder selection. The lowest quote on an underspecified project is the most expensive option — paid in rebuilds. Select on communication quality and relevant references, not price.

**The test:** if the agency vanished tomorrow, could your team run and extend everything? If not, you do not have a product — you have a dependency. Plan the in-housing from the start.
