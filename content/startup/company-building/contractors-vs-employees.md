Early teams constantly choose between **contractors** (flexible, fast, no long-term commitment) and **employees** (committed, cultural, expensive to unwind). Contractors suit bounded, well-specified work with an end date; employees suit the core that compounds — product direction, architecture, customer relationships.

```text
contractors:  fast start, easy stop, pay per output — weak loyalty, context walks out
employees:    slow start, hard stop, pay always — compound context, need management
```

The legal lines matter more than founders expect: misclassified employees (contractors in everything but name) create tax and labor liability, and contractor-created IP needs explicit assignment ([contractor agreement](/t/contractor-agreement)). Get the paperwork right for both.

The classic mistakes:

- Contractors for the core. The product's heart built by rotating freelancers means architecture lives in nobody's head and every handoff relearns. Core team = employees; edges = contractors.
- Treating contractors as cheap employees. Lower hourly cost with none of the commitment cuts both ways — availability, loyalty and context all priced accordingly. Compare fully-loaded, honest math.
- Misclassification. Full-time hours, company equipment, exclusivity, managed schedule — walk and quack like employment, and regulators will call it that, with back taxes attached. If it looks like a job, hire for it.
- No endgame defined. Contractors drifting for years without converting or leaving accumulate dependency without commitment. Set review points: convert, extend with reason, or end.

**Default:** employees for the compounding core, contractors for spikes and specialties. Write both relationships properly from day one — the paperwork is cheap, the cleanup is not.
