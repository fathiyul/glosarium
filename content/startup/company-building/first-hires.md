**First hires** (roughly employees 1–10) define the company more than any later cohort: they set quality bars, working norms and culture by example, cover impossible breadth, and their strengths and gaps shape everything built on top. Hire slowly here — each of the first ten is ~10% of the company.

```text
hire for:  slope over y-intercept (learning speed > current skill) · versatility · ownership
           · culture addition (not just fit) · belief in the mission (discount accepted knowingly)
```

Prioritize multipliers: people who raise the bar, attract the next hires, and operate without management overhead. Generalists who learn beat specialists waiting for perfect scope at this stage — there is no perfect scope yet.

The classic mistakes:

- Hiring for the company you wish you were. Senior specialists needing structure, juniors needing mentorship you cannot give. Hire for the chaos you have: self-directed generalists who ship.
- Selling a job, not the mission. Candidates joining for salary parity leave at the first hard quarter. Recruit believers at a deliberate discount (made fair with [equity](/t/employee-stock-options)) — mercenaries churn expensively.
- Skipping reference depth. One casual chat passes; structured back-channel references reveal patterns (brilliant jerk? flaky finisher?). Check thoroughly — early mis-hires cost quarters.
- Cloning the founders. Same backgrounds, same blind spots, same networks. Diversity of thought and experience at hire 3 prevents monoculture that later requires painful correction.
- No onboarding at all. "Figure it out" wastes months of expensive hires' ramp. Lightweight onboarding (buddy, first-week wins, context docs) pays back immediately ([onboarding others](/t/onboarding-others)).

**The bar:** every early hire should raise the average. If the choice is between a fast maybe and waiting for a hell-yes, wait — the cost of a mis-hire at this stage dwarfs the cost of the delay.
