A **founding engineer** (typically hires 1–5) owns enormous scope: architecture decisions that last years, hiring bar for the team behind them, culture of engineering quality — in exchange for meaningful equity and a title reflecting the bet. They join for the mission and the upside, accepting startup chaos over big-company comfort.

```text
gets:    broad ownership · outsized equity · direct founder access · career acceleration
gives:   tolerance for chaos · generalist range · recruiting help · cultural foundation
```

Hire for slope and range over specialization: the stack will change, the product will pivot, and only fast-learning generalists survive both. Prioritize builders with opinions (who will argue with founders productively) over executors waiting for specs.

The classic mistakes:

- Hiring specialists too early. The Kubernetes expert with nothing to orchestrate yet, idle and expensive. Generalists first; specialists when the problem they solve is proven painful.
- Junior equity, senior expectations. Founding-engineer scope with standard-hire grants breeds resentment by year two. Price the risk honestly upfront — meaningful equity or market cash, never neither.
- No growth path defined. The generalist who built v1 watches specialists arrive above them with no plan discussed. Talk levels, leadership paths and evolving roles early and often.
- Founders never letting go. Reviewing every diff and deciding every stack choice at hire ten strangles the ownership you hired for. Delegate architecture progressively; keep vetoes for truly irreversible calls.
- Skipping the mission sell. Top founding engineers choose teams, not salaries. Sell the problem, the people and the growth — then close with fair economics ([compensation](/t/startup-compensation)).

**Retain deliberately:** refresh grants before cliffs, grow titles with scope, and keep the direct founder relationship that money cannot replace. Founding engineers who stay become the engineering culture.
