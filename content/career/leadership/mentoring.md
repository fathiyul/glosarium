**Mentoring** is helping a less experienced person **grow**, by sharing experience, perspective and feedback, and by creating opportunities for them to learn. It's one of the most valuable things a senior engineer can do. It multiplies a team's capability far more than your own individual output, and it's an expected part of growing in scope ([scope of work](/t/scope-of-work), [impact](/t/impact)). It also teaches you, since explaining forces clarity.

## Related roles

| Role | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Mentor** | A more experienced person sharing guidance, context and perspective, often over a longer relationship |
| **Coach** | Helps someone find their own answers through questions |
| **Sponsor** | Uses their influence to create opportunities for someone (a stretch assignment, a recommendation) |
| **Buddy** | A peer who helps a newcomer settle in ([first weeks](/t/first-weeks)) |

## What good mentoring looks like

- **Start with their goals.** What do they want to learn or become? A short conversation about goals and current strengths shapes everything. Revisit it occasionally.
- **Meet regularly**, even briefly (weekly or fortnightly), so the relationship doesn't depend on crises ([one-on-ones](/t/one-on-one)).
- **Give context, not just instructions.** Explain *why* decisions are made, what trade-offs you weighed, how the system evolved. Context is what juniors lack.
- **Let them struggle productively**, then help. Don't solve it for them in the first minute. Ask "what have you tried?" and "what do you think is happening?", and give hints before answers ([productive struggle](/t/productive-struggle), [when to ask for help](/t/when-to-ask-for-help)).
- **Delegate real work with support.** Stretch tasks slightly beyond comfort, with a safety net: check-ins, a design review, and your availability. People grow by doing.
- **Review their work to teach**, not just to correct. Explain the reasoning behind comments ([giving code review](/t/giving-code-review)).
- **Give specific, timely, kind feedback**, including praise for what's going well ([giving feedback](/t/giving-feedback)).
- **Pair on hard problems** and let them observe how you think: how you debug, how you read an unfamiliar codebase ([pairing](/t/pairing)).
- **Share your mistakes** and uncertainty. It normalizes learning and makes it safe to ask ([psychological safety](/t/psychological-safety)).
- **Point to resources and people**, not only to yourself. Introduce them around.
- **Advocate for them**: make their work visible to others, and recommend them for opportunities.

## Pitfalls

- **Doing it for them.** Taking over because it's faster. It helps today and hurts their growth.
- **Mentoring in your own image.** Support their path and style, not a copy of yours.
- **Only criticizing**, or only reassuring.
- **Unclear expectations** about time and topics. Agree on them upfront.
- **Neglecting it under pressure.** It's real work. Make time for it, and make it visible to your manager.
- **Overstepping**: you advise, they decide. Respect confidentiality too.
- **Assuming one approach fits everyone.** People learn differently.

## If you're the junior

You can ask for mentoring, and make it easier: prepare topics, bring specific questions, act on advice and report back, and respect their time ([finding a mentor](/t/finding-a-mentor)).

The best signs that mentoring works: the person needs you **less over time**, takes on bigger problems and starts helping others.
